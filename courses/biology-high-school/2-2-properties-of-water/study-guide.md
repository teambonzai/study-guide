## 1. Water is polar

**Water is polar: one end is a little negative, and the other end is a little positive.**

**What it is:**

- A water molecule (**H₂O**) has one oxygen and two hydrogens.
- **Electrons** are pulled toward the oxygen.
- **Outside source:** An **electron** is a tiny particle with a negative charge. A static shock from a doorknob is electrons jumping. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- So the **oxygen** has a **partial negative charge** (**δ−**).
- The two **hydrogens** have a **partial positive charge** (**δ+**).
- The δ sign stands for "partial." The slide puts δ− next to "Partial negative charge."

**Numbers on the slide:**

- The angle between the two hydrogens is **104.45°** (degrees).
- Each oxygen-to-hydrogen bond is **95.84 pm** long.
- **Outside source:** **pm** stands for **picometer**, a super tiny unit of length. One picometer is one trillionth of a meter. A baseball bat is about one meter long. Cut it into a trillion equal pieces, and each piece is one picometer. *(NIST)*

**In the picture:**

- The left picture draws water in three ways.
- **Basic geometric structure:** letters and lines. It shows the angle and the length.
- **Ball-and-stick model:** balls joined by sticks.
- **Space-filling model:** the balls are pressed together.
- In both models, the red ball is oxygen. The white balls are hydrogen.
- The right picture says "Electrons are pulled toward oxygen."
- Its top label is "Partial negative charge," next to the oxygen.
- Its bottom label is "Partial positive charge," next to the hydrogens.

> 📷 See **Slide 2** — left: three ways to draw water, with 104.45° and 95.84 pm. Right: electrons pulled toward the red oxygen.

## 2. Hydrogen bonds

**A hydrogen bond is a pull between two molecules.**

**What it is:**

- The slide says: "Hydrogen from one molecule attracted to oxygen atom on another molecule."
- The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is pulled to the δ− oxygen of another.
- It is an **intermolecular force**. The slide underlines "Inter."
- **Outside source:** The prefix **inter** means between. An *interstate* highway runs between states. So an intermolecular force is a force between molecules. *(Merriam-Webster)*
- Hydrogen bonds happen with other molecules too, not just water.
- They can involve nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine. It is not just oxygen.

**In the pictures:**

- The bottom drawing shows three water molecules with δ+ and δ− signs.
- Its red dotted lines are labeled **hydrogen bonding**.
- They run from one molecule to another molecule.
- Its solid lines are labeled **intramolecular polar covalent bonding**.
- The solid lines join the O and H inside one molecule.
- **Outside source:** The prefix **intra** means inside. *Intramural* sports are games played inside one school. So an intramolecular bond is inside one molecule. *(Merriam-Webster)*
- **Outside source:** In a **polar covalent bond**, two atoms share electrons, but not evenly. One atom pulls the shared electrons closer. It's like two kids sharing a blanket, and one tugs harder. In water, oxygen pulls harder than hydrogen. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- The right picture shows four water molecules.
- Its blue dotted lines are labeled **Hydrogen bonds**.
- Each dotted line links a δ+ hydrogen to a δ− oxygen.
- The middle molecule is bonded to three other molecules at once.

> 📷 See **Slide 3** — dotted lines are hydrogen bonds between molecules. Solid lines are bonds inside one molecule.

## 3. Cohesion and adhesion

**Water sticks to itself, and it also sticks to other things.**

**What it is:**

- **Cohesion** is attraction to one another. For water, it means water sticking to water.
- **Adhesion** is attraction to a different substance. It means water sticking to something else.
- The slide's memory hook: "Co" = together. "Ad" = between.

**In the pictures:**

- Cartoon: two water molecules shake hands. The handshake is labeled **Cohesion**.
- The same molecules grab a rope on each side. Each grip is labeled **Adhesion**.
- Plant picture: a chain of molecules, each labeled **Water molecule**, sits next to a brown wall labeled **Xylem wall**.
- **Outside source:** Xylem are tiny tubes inside a plant. They carry water up from the roots to the leaves. The strings in a celery stalk contain them. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- Links between the molecules are labeled **Cohesion**.
- The link from a molecule to the xylem wall is labeled **Adhesion**.
- The dotted links are labeled **Hydrogen bonds**.
- Droplet picture: a **water droplet** sits on a flat surface.
- Two zoom circles show the molecules inside the drop up close.
- One circle is at the drop's edge. The other is in the middle.
- Arrows point from each molecule toward the molecules around it.

> 📷 See **Slide 4** — cartoon molecules shake hands (cohesion) and grab ropes (adhesion).

## 4. Capillary action

**Capillary action is water rising up a thin tube.**

**What it is:**

- The slide calls it "the tendency of water to rise in a thin tube."
- It is caused by cohesion **and** adhesion.
- **Outside source:** Adhesion pulls water up along the tube's walls. Cohesion pulls the rest of the water up behind it. A paper towel dipped in a spill soaks water upward this way. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the pictures:**

- The drawing shows a thin **capillary tube** standing in a container of water.
- Its label says "water moves upward by capillary action."
- The water inside the tube is higher than the water outside.
- The curved top of the water in the tube is labeled **meniscus**.
- The photo shows glass tubes of different widths, joined at the bottom.
- The thinner the tube, the higher the blue water climbs.

> 📷 See **Slide 5** — water climbs inside a thin tube. In the photo, the thinnest tube has the highest water.

## 5. Surface tension

**Surface tension means the top of the water resists an outside force.**

**What it is:**

- The slide says it is the "tendency of the surface of water to resist an external force."
- **Outside source:** **External** means from outside. An external force is a push or pull from outside, like your finger poking the water. *(Merriam-Webster)*
- It is caused by **cohesion**.

**In the pictures:**

- The photo shows an insect standing on water without sinking.
- The droplet drawing labels the **air**, the **water droplet**, and the **water molecules**.
- A **surface molecule is pulled inward**.
- An **inner molecule is pulled in all directions**.
- The drawing says "Diagram not to scale." (The sizes are not real sizes.)
- The other drawing shows a ball pressing a dip into the water's surface.
- At the surface, cohesive pulls are **stronger**.
- That is because of an "imbalance of forces" there.
- Surface molecules get pulled more strongly into the **bulk**.
- **Outside source:** The **bulk** is the main body of the water. In a pool, the bulk is all the water under the top layer. *(Merriam-Webster)*
- In the bulk, cohesive pulls are **weaker**.
- That is because each molecule there feels pulls from all directions.

> 📷 See **Slide 6** — an insect standing on the water, and molecules pulled inward at the surface.

## 6. Specific heat capacity

**Water takes a lot of energy to heat up, so it warms slowly.**

**What it is:**

- **Specific heat capacity** is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of water by 1°C.
- Water absorbs heat without getting hot quickly.

**Why it matters:**

- Water resists fast temperature change.
- This keeps climates and body temperatures steady.
- It helps living things keep **homeostasis**.
- **Outside source:** **Homeostasis** means keeping the inside of a living thing steady. Your body stays near the same temperature on hot and cold days. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Numbers on the slide:**

| Material | Symbol | Specific heat |
|---|---|---|
| Copper block | Cu | 390 J/kg°C |
| Aluminum block | Al | 910 J/kg°C |
| Water | H₂O | 4200 J/kg°C |

- Copper has the lowest number. Water has the highest.
- Water's number is more than 10 times copper's number.
- **Outside source:** **J/kg°C** means joules per kilogram per degree Celsius. Each number is the joules needed to warm 1 kilogram by 1°C. It's like a price tag for warming things up. *(NIST)*
- **Outside source:** A **joule (J)** is a unit of energy. Lifting an apple one meter off a table takes about one joule. *(NIST)*
- **Outside source:** A **kilogram (kg)** is a unit of mass. A one-liter bottle of water has a mass of about one kilogram. *(NIST)*

**In the picture:**

- A green arrow runs from lower specific heat to higher specific heat.
- **Lower specific heat capacity:** warms up and cools down quickly.
- That is because it takes much less energy to change its temperature.
- **Higher specific heat capacity:** warms up and cools down slowly.
- That is because it takes much more energy to change its temperature.

> 📷 See **Slide 7** — copper (390) and aluminum (910) warm up fast. Water (4200) warms up slowly.

## 7. Hydrophilic, hydrophobic, and amphipathic

**Some things mix with water, some don't, and some molecules do both.**

The slide's title is "H2O Polarity Characteristics."

| Word | Slide's meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrophilic** | "Water-loving." Dissolves easily in water. | Salts, sugars |
| **Hydrophobic** | "Water-fearing." Does not dissolve in water. | Oils, fats |
| **Amphipathic** | Has both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. | Soap bubbles |

- **Outside source:** To **dissolve** means to break apart and spread evenly through a liquid. Sugar stirred into tea dissolves and disappears. *(OpenStax Chemistry 2e)*

**More about amphipathic:**

- An amphipathic molecule has both hydrophilic and hydrophobic **regions** (parts).
- It allows formation of **cell membranes**.
- **Outside source:** A **cell membrane** is the thin outer layer around a cell. It works like the skin of a grape. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- The slide calls cell membranes **phospholipid bilayers**.
- **Outside source:** "Bi" means two, so a **bilayer** is two layers. A *bicycle* has two wheels. *(Merriam-Webster)*
- It enables molecules to interact with both water and **lipids**.
- **Outside source:** **Lipids** include fats and oils. Butter and cooking oil are lipids. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the picture:**

- The picture is titled **Phospholipid Structure**.
- A **phospholipid** is the molecule in a phospholipid bilayer.
- The round blue top is labeled **Hydrophilic head**.
- The two wavy orange lines are labeled **Hydrophobic tail**.

> 📷 See **Slide 8** — a phospholipid has a water-loving head and water-fearing tails.

## 8. Suspensions and emulsions

**Suspensions and emulsions are two kinds of mixtures.**

The slide's title is "Liquid Forms." It covers suspensions, emulsions, and density.

**Suspension:**

- A mixture where large particles float in water but do not dissolve.
- Example: blood cells in plasma.
- **Outside source:** **Plasma** is the liquid part of blood. It is mostly water. A bag of donated blood holds cells floating in plasma. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- Example: sand in water.

**Emulsion:**

- A mixture of two liquids that don't normally mix.
- One liquid is **dispersed** (spread out in tiny bits) in the other.
- Example: oil droplets in water.
- Example: bile emulsifying fats.
- **Outside source:** **Bile** is a digestive juice your body makes. **Emulsifying** means breaking fat into tiny drops that spread through water. Dish soap does this to grease on a pan. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> 📷 See **Slide 9** — the top half of the slide lists both mixtures with examples.

## 9. Density and why ice floats

**Ice floats because it is less dense than liquid water.**

**What it is:**

- **Density** is mass per unit volume: **D = m / V**.
- Water is most dense at **4°C**.
- Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats.
- **Outside source:** **Mass** is how much matter something has. A bowling ball has more mass than a beach ball. *(OpenStax Chemistry 2e)*
- **Outside source:** **Volume** is how much space something takes up. A beach ball has more volume than a baseball. *(OpenStax Chemistry 2e)*

**In the picture:**

- The picture writes the formula as **ρ = M / V**.
- Arrows label **density** (ρ), **mass** (M), and **Volume** (V).
- So ρ = M / V means the same thing as D = m / V.
- **Outside source:** **ρ** is the Greek letter "rho." Like π (pi) in math class, it is a Greek letter used as a symbol. *(OpenStax Chemistry 2e)*

> 📷 See **Slide 9** — the right picture labels each letter in ρ = M / V.

## Quick review

- Oxygen is partial negative. The hydrogens are partial positive.
- Water's bond angle is 104.45°. Each bond is 95.84 pm long.
- Water can be drawn as a geometric structure, a ball-and-stick model, or a space-filling model.
- Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. Polar covalent bonds are inside one molecule.
- Hydrogen bonds can involve nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine.
- Cohesion = water to water. Adhesion = water to something else.
- Capillary action = cohesion + adhesion. The thinnest tube has the highest water.
- Surface tension = cohesion. Surface molecules are pulled inward.
- Specific heat: copper 390, aluminum 910, water 4200 J/kg°C.
- Lower specific heat warms and cools quickly. Higher warms and cools slowly.
- Hydrophilic dissolves. Hydrophobic doesn't. Amphipathic has both parts.
- A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail.
- A suspension has bits that don't dissolve. An emulsion has liquids that don't mix.
- Density = mass ÷ volume (D = m / V, or ρ = M / V).
- Water is densest at 4°C, and ice floats.
