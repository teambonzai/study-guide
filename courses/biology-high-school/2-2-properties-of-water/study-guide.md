## 1. Water is polar 🎯

**Water is polar: one end is a little negative, and the other end is a little positive.**

- A water molecule (**H₂O**) has one oxygen and two hydrogens.
- **Electrons** (tiny negative particles) are pulled toward the oxygen.
- So the **oxygen** is partial negative (**δ−**).
- The two **hydrogens** are partial positive (**δ+**). The δ sign means "partial."

> **Analogy:** Two kids share a blanket, but one kid keeps pulling it closer. Oxygen is that kid. The electrons are the blanket.

## 2. Hydrogen bonds 🎯

**A hydrogen bond is a pull between two molecules.**

- The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is pulled to the δ− oxygen of another.
- It is an **intermolecular force**. That means a force *between* molecules.
- It happens in other molecules too, not just water.
- It can involve nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine.

> **Analogy:** Think of magnets. The + end of one snaps toward the − end of the next.

> 📷 See **Slide 3** — dotted lines are hydrogen bonds between molecules. The line inside one molecule is a different bond. It is called a polar covalent bond. It holds one water molecule together.

## 3. Cohesion and adhesion 🎯

**Water sticks to itself, and it also sticks to other things.**

- **Cohesion** means water sticking to water.
- **Adhesion** means water sticking to a different substance.
- The slide's memory hook: "Co" = together. "Ad" = between.
- In a plant, water sticks to itself and to the **xylem** walls. Xylem is the tubes that carry water up *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*.

> **Analogy:** Cohesion is classmates holding hands. Adhesion is a student holding the stair rail.

> 📷 See **Slide 4** — cartoon water molecules hold hands (cohesion) and grab ropes (adhesion).

---

## 4. Capillary action 🎯

**Capillary action is water rising up a thin tube.**

- It is caused by cohesion **and** adhesion working together.
- Adhesion pulls water up along the tube's walls.
- Cohesion pulls the rest of the water up behind it.
- The thinner the tube, the higher the water climbs.

> 📷 See **Slide 5** — water climbs inside a thin tube. Its curved top is called the **meniscus**. In the photo, the thinnest tube has the highest water.

---

## 5. Surface tension 🎯

**Surface tension means the top of the water resists an outside force.**

- It is caused by cohesion **only**.
- A molecule deep in the water is pulled in all directions.
- A molecule at the surface is pulled inward.
- So the surface holds together like a stretchy skin.

> **Analogy:** A trampoline bends when you press on it, but it doesn't tear.

> 📷 See **Slide 6** — a water strider (an insect) stands on water without sinking.

## 6. Specific heat capacity 🎯

**Water takes a lot of energy to heat up, so it warms slowly.**

- **Specific heat capacity** is the energy needed to raise the temperature by 1°C.
- Water's specific heat is high. It soaks up heat without getting hot quickly.
- This keeps climates and body temperatures steady.
- It helps living things keep **homeostasis** (a steady inside).

> **Analogy:** On a sunny day, a metal slide gets too hot to touch. The pool next to it stays cool.

> 📷 See **Slide 7** — copper and aluminum warm up fast. Water warms up slowly. ⛔ Skip the exact numbers.

## 7. Hydrophilic, hydrophobic, and amphipathic 🎯

**Some things mix with water, some don't, and some molecules do both.**

| Word | Plain meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrophilic** | Water-loving. Dissolves easily in water. | Salts, sugars |
| **Hydrophobic** | Water-fearing. Does not dissolve in water. | Oils, fats |
| **Amphipathic** | Has a water-loving part and a water-fearing part. | Soap bubbles |

- Amphipathic molecules can work with both water and **lipids** (fats and oils).
- They build **cell membranes**, the thin outer layer of a cell.

> 📷 See **Slide 8** — a **phospholipid** has a water-loving head and water-fearing tails.

---

## 8. Suspensions and emulsions 🎯

**Suspensions and emulsions are two kinds of mixtures.**

- **Suspension:** big bits are in water, but they do not dissolve.
- Examples: sand in water, or blood cells in **plasma** (the liquid part of blood) *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*.
- **Emulsion:** two liquids that don't normally mix. One spreads out in the other.
- Examples: oil drops in water, or **bile** (a digestive juice) breaking up fats *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*.

> **Analogy:** Shake a bottle of oil-and-vinegar dressing. The oil breaks into tiny drops. That's an emulsion.

> 📷 See **Slide 9** — the top half of the slide lists both mixtures with examples.

## 9. Density and why ice floats 🎯

**Ice floats because it is less dense than liquid water.**

- **Density** is mass per unit volume: **D = m / V**.
- Mass is how much matter something has. Volume is how much space it takes up.
- Water is most dense at **4°C**.
- Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats.

> **Why:** Floating ice stays on top of a pond. The water below stays liquid, so fish can live through winter *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*.

> 📷 See **Slide 9** — the picture uses ρ ("rho") for density: ρ = M / V. It means the same as D = m / V.

## Quick review

- Oxygen is partial negative. The hydrogens are partial positive.
- Hydrogen bonds pull water molecules toward each other.
- Cohesion = water to water. Adhesion = water to something else.
- Capillary action = cohesion + adhesion. Surface tension = cohesion only.
- Water heats up slowly, so it keeps temperatures steady.
- Hydrophilic dissolves. Hydrophobic doesn't. Amphipathic has both parts.
- A suspension has bits that don't dissolve. An emulsion has liquids that don't mix.
- Water is densest at 4°C, and ice floats.
