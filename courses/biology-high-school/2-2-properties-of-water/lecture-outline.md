## Slide 2 — The water molecule
- Water (H₂O) is **polar**.
- Electrons are pulled toward oxygen, so **oxygen is partial negative** (δ−).
- The **hydrogens are partial positive** (δ+).
- Angle between the hydrogens: **104.45°**.
- Each oxygen-to-hydrogen bond: **95.84 pm** long.
- Three drawings: **basic geometric structure**, **ball-and-stick model**, **space-filling model**.
- In the models, red = oxygen and white = hydrogen.

## Slide 3 — Hydrogen bonds
- A hydrogen on one molecule is attracted to an oxygen on another.
- It is an **intermolecular force** (the slide underlines "Inter").
- It happens with other molecules too. It can involve nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine.
- Picture: dotted lines = **hydrogen bonds**, each linking δ+ H to δ− O.
- Picture: solid lines = **intramolecular polar covalent bonding** (inside one molecule).
- Picture: the middle molecule bonds to three others.

## Slide 4 — Cohesion and adhesion
- **Cohesion** = attraction to one another (water sticking to water). "Co" = together.
- **Adhesion** = attraction to a different substance. "Ad" = between.
- Cartoon: handshake between molecules = cohesion. Grabbing ropes = adhesion.
- Plant picture: **Water molecule** chain beside a **Xylem wall**. Molecule-to-molecule = cohesion, molecule-to-xylem wall = adhesion. Links are hydrogen bonds.
- Droplet picture: zoom circles at the edge and middle of a water drop.

## Slide 5 — Capillary action
- The tendency of water to rise in a thin tube.
- Caused by **cohesion and adhesion**.
- Drawing: a **capillary tube** in water. Water moves upward by capillary action.
- The curved top of the water is the **meniscus**.
- Photo: the thinnest tube has the highest water.

## Slide 6 — Surface tension
- The water's surface resists an **external** (outside) force.
- Caused by **cohesion**.
- Photo: an insect stands on the water.
- **Surface molecule** is pulled inward. **Inner molecule** is pulled in all directions.
- Stronger cohesive pulls at the surface, from an imbalance of forces. Pulled into the **bulk**.
- Weaker cohesive pulls in the bulk, since pulls come from all directions.
- Labels: air, water droplet, water molecules. "Diagram not to scale."
- A ball presses a dip into the surface.

## Slide 7 — Specific heat capacity
- Energy needed to raise the temperature of water by 1°C.
- Water resists fast temperature change. This keeps climates and body temperatures steady.
- Water absorbs heat without getting hot quickly. This helps **homeostasis**.
- Copper (Cu) = **390 J/kg°C**. Aluminum (Al) = **910 J/kg°C**. Water = **4200 J/kg°C**.
- **Lower** specific heat: warms and cools **quickly** (needs much less energy).
- **Higher** specific heat: warms and cools **slowly** (needs much more energy).

## Slide 8 — Hydrophilic, hydrophobic, amphipathic ("H2O Polarity Characteristics")
- **Hydrophilic** = water-loving, dissolves easily (salts, sugars).
- **Hydrophobic** = water-fearing, doesn't dissolve (oils, fats).
- **Amphipathic** = has hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions (soap bubbles).
- Amphipathic molecules allow cell membranes (**phospholipid bilayers**) to form.
- They let molecules interact with both water and lipids.
- Picture "Phospholipid Structure": **hydrophilic head**, **hydrophobic tail**.

## Slide 9 — Suspensions and emulsions ("Liquid Forms")
- **Suspension** = large particles float in water but don't dissolve.
- Suspension examples: blood cells in plasma, sand in water.
- **Emulsion** = two liquids that don't normally mix. One is dispersed in the other.
- Emulsion examples: oil droplets in water, bile emulsifying fats.

## Slide 9 — Density
- **Density** = mass per unit volume (D = m / V).
- Picture: **ρ = M / V**, with density (ρ), mass (M), and Volume (V) labeled.
- Water is most dense at **4°C**.
- Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats.
