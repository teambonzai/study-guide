## 1. Proteins and their jobs

**Proteins do many jobs, and a protein's shape decides its job.**

**Jobs on the slide:**
- Regulate.
- Transport.
- Structure.
- Control reaction rates.
- The slide lists **enzymes** under "Control reaction rates."
- **Outside source:** To regulate means to control how something works. To transport means to carry something from place to place. Example: a thermostat regulates a room's heat. Example: a school bus transports students. *(Merriam-Webster Dictionary)*
- **Outside source:** An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction in your body. Example: an enzyme in your saliva starts breaking down bread while you chew. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- The slide says **"STRUCTURE = FUNCTION!"** A protein's shape decides the job it can do.

> **Analogy:** A key opens a lock because of its shape. Bend the key, and it stops working.

## 2. Amino acids: the building blocks

**Proteins are built from small pieces called amino acids.**

**What it is:**
- The slide says **"Monomer = amino acids."**
- **Outside source:** A monomer is one small building block. Many monomers link together into a chain. Example: one Lego brick is like a monomer. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- There are about **20** different amino acids.

**The amino acid drawing:**
- On the left is an N with two H's.
- On the right is a C with an O and an OH.
- At the bottom is an R.
- These all attach to one C in the middle. That C also has an H on top.
- On the slide, green boxes cover three labels. The full-size picture shows all three.
- **Amino group:** the N with two H's.
- **Carboxyl group:** the C with an O and an OH.
- **Side chain:** the R at the bottom.
- The slide says the **R-group / side chain "differs!"** It is different in each amino acid.

**The chart of 20 amino acids:**
- The chart sorts amino acids by their side chains.
- **Nonpolar side chains (9):** glycine (G, Gly), alanine (A, Ala), valine (V, Val).
- More nonpolar: leucine (L, Leu), isoleucine (I, Ile), methionine (M, Met).
- More nonpolar: phenylalanine (F, Phe), tryptophan (W, Trp), proline (P, Pro).
- **Polar side chains (6):** serine (S, Ser), threonine (T, Thr), cysteine (C, Cys).
- More polar: tyrosine (Y, Tyr), asparagine (N, Asn), glutamine (Q, Gln).
- **Electrically charged side chains:** 5 amino acids, split into two groups.
- **Acidic (2):** aspartate (D, Asp) and glutamate (E, Glu).
- **Basic (3):** lysine (K, Lys), arginine (R, Arg), and histidine (H, His).
- Each amino acid has a name, a one-letter code, and a three-letter code. Example: Glycine (G), Gly.
- Every drawing has the same top part. Only the shaded side chain changes.

## 3. From amino acid to protein

**Amino acids join into chains, and chains fold into proteins.**

- **Peptide:** a short chain of about **2–50** amino acids.
- **Polypeptide:** a longer chain of about **51 or more** amino acids.
- **Protein:** **1 or more** polypeptides.
- A protein has a **3D structure**, and it is **functional**.
- **Outside source:** Functional means able to do its job. Example: a phone that turns on and makes calls is functional. *(Merriam-Webster Dictionary)*
- The slide uses "aa" as a short way to write "amino acid."

> **Analogy:** Amino acids are like beads. String a few, and you get a short chain. String many, and you get a long chain.

> 📷 See **Slide 2** — arrows go from Amino Acid to Polypeptide to Protein. The bottom strip shows one amino acid, then a peptide, then a protein.

## 4. The four levels of protein structure

**A protein's shape is built in four levels. Each level builds on the one before.**

- In order, the levels are **primary**, **secondary**, **tertiary**, and **quaternary**.
- Every protein has the first three levels *(Slide 6 notes)*.
- Only proteins with two or more chains have the fourth level *(Slide 6 notes)*.

**In the picture:**
- Primary: a chain of beads. The beads are labeled amino acids.
- Secondary: a blue coil (**α-helix**) and a red wavy strip (**β-pleated sheet**).
- Tertiary: one chain folded into a blob. It still has α-helices and a β-pleated sheet inside.
- Quaternary: folded chains packed together.

> **Analogy:** Think of writing. Letters make words. Words make sentences. Sentences make paragraphs. *(Slide 10)*

## 5. Level 1 — Primary structure

**Primary structure is the order of amino acids in the chain.**

**What it is:**
- Amino acids lined up in a certain order.
- Each chain has its own set of amino acids in a certain order *(Slide 4 notes)*.

**Key fact:**
- **Peptide bonds** join the amino acids.
- A peptide bond is **covalent**.
- **Outside source:** Covalent means the atoms share electrons. Sharing makes the bond strong and hard to break. Example: the bonds inside one water molecule are covalent too. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- Peptide bonds form by **dehydration synthesis**. Two pieces join, and water is given off.

**Chain ends:**
- The left picture labels one end **N-terminus** and the other end **C-terminus**.
- The right chart labels its two ends **Amino end** and **Carboxyl end**.

**From the notes:**
- The DNA of a gene sets the order of amino acids.
- The notes add: the gene may code for only part of a protein.
- That happens in proteins made of more than one subunit (piece).
- A change in the gene's DNA can change the amino acid order.
- Changing just one amino acid can change the protein's shape and job.
- **Outside source:** A gene is a part of DNA. It holds the code for making a protein. Example: a gene is like a recipe, and one typo can ruin the cake. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the picture:**
- The big chain is labeled N-terminus at one end and C-terminus at the other.
- A zoom shows four amino acids: Leu, Phe, Ser, and Cys. (The slide spells Ser as "Set.")
- A pink box points to the **peptide bond** between two amino acids.
- A circle zooms in on one amino acid. Its side chain is shaded.
- The right chart shows three amino acids (aa₁, aa₂, aa₃) joining.
- An OH and an H come off at each joint. They leave as water.
- The result shows two peptide bonds and "+ 2(H₂O)." That means two waters were given off.

> **Analogy:** Level 1 is like **letters**. Order matters. Change one letter, and "cat" becomes "cut."

> 📷 See **Slide 4** — the chain is on the left. The joining chart is on the right.

## 6. Level 2 — Secondary structure

**Secondary structure is small folds in the chain, like coils and pleated sheets.**

**What it is:**
- Small folded shapes that form in parts of one chain *(Slide 5 notes)*.
- The slide says the most common ones are the α helix and the β pleated sheet.
- The **α helix** (alpha helix) is drawn as a coil, like a spring.
- The **β pleated sheet** (beta pleated sheet) is drawn as a zigzag, like a folded paper fan.

**Key fact:**
- The slide says they are **"Caused by hydrogen bonds!"**
- **Outside source:** A hydrogen bond is a weak pull between atoms. Example: hydrogen bonds pull water molecules together, so water forms drops. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**From the notes:**
- These folds come from pulls between atoms of the **backbone**.
- The backbone is the chain itself, without the R-groups.
- So side chains are not part of secondary structure.
- Each hydrogen bond links two amino acids.
- It forms between the **carbonyl O** of one amino acid and the **amino H** of another.
- **Outside source:** Carbonyl O is the O in a C=O group. Amino H is the H in an N–H group. Example: nail polish remover also has a C=O group. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the picture:**
- Simple drawings show a beta sheet (zigzag) and an alpha helix (coil).
- The middle drawing shows the atoms. Red dotted lines link each C=O to an H–N.
- Those red dotted lines are the hydrogen bonds.
- The right drawing labels the hydrogen bonds with dashed lines.
- The full-size middle picture has a caption. It says the beta sheet has two amino acid chains side by side.
- The caption says the alpha helix has coiling.
- It says the red dots are hydrogen bonds. They hold together both the beta pleated sheets and the alpha helix.

> **Analogy:** Level 2 is like **words**. Letters group into words. The chain groups into coils and sheets.

> 📷 See **Slide 5** — look for the dotted lines on the coil and the zigzag.

## 7. Level 3 — Tertiary structure

**Tertiary structure is the whole 3D shape of one folded chain.**

**What it is:**
- The full **3D shape** of one polypeptide.
- Every protein has this level *(Slide 6 notes)*.

**Key fact:**
- The slide says it is "due to interactions between amino acid side chains."
- That means side chains pull on each other to make the shape.

**The four side-chain interactions on the slide:**
- **Ionic bond:** the picture shows a + and a − side by side.
- **Outside source:** An ionic bond is a pull between a plus (+) charge and a minus (−) charge. Opposite charges attract, like the ends of two magnets. Example: table salt is held together by ionic bonds. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- **Hydrogen bond:** the picture shows an OH on one side chain linked to an O on another.
- **Disulfide bridge:** the picture shows it as S–S, linking two parts of the chain.
- **Outside source:** S is the symbol for sulfur. A disulfide bridge links two sulfur atoms, like a staple. Example: links like these help hold the shape of your hair. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- **Hydrophobic interaction:** the picture shows two side chains meeting in a pink box.
- Section 8 explains the word "hydrophobic."

**In the full-size left picture:**
- The drawing of the four interactions is labeled "Types of side chain interactions."
- The disulfide bridge is drawn as CH₂–S–S–CH₂.
- An arrow points to the folded ribbon, labeled "Overall 3D shape (3° Structure)."

**From the notes:**
- The notes call tertiary structure the "overall three-dimensional structure of a polypeptide."
- It is mostly due to pulls between the R-groups.
- All proteins have primary, secondary, and tertiary structures.
- Quaternary structure only happens when a protein has two or more chains.

> **Analogy:** Level 3 is like a **sentence**. Words come together into one complete thought.

> 📷 See **Slide 6** — each interaction is labeled in pink where two side chains meet. The ribbon on the left is a folded chain.

## 8. The hydrophobic effect

**In water, a chain folds so its nonpolar amino acids gather in the middle.**

**What the words mean:**
- **Outside source:** Hydrophobic means "water-fearing." Hydrophobic things don't mix with water, so they clump together. Example: oil forms drops in salad dressing. Example: rain beads up on a waxed car. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- **Outside source:** Polar things mix with water. Nonpolar things do not. Example: sugar (polar) dissolves in tea, but oil (nonpolar) floats on water. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**What happens:**
- The unfolded chain has polar and nonpolar amino acids mixed along it.
- A blue layer around the chain is labeled **hydration layer**.
- **Outside source:** Hydration means water. The hydration layer is the water around the chain. Example: it is like the thin coat of water on a washed grape. *(Merriam-Webster Dictionary)*
- During **protein folding**, nonpolar amino acids gather in the **center**.
- Polar amino acids stay on the **outside**, near the water.

**The slide's label says two things:**
- Hydrophobic effects make the hydration layer smaller.
- They help weak interactions form between amino acid **residues**.
- **Outside source:** A residue is one amino acid inside a chain. Example: it is like one bead on a necklace. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the pictures:**
- Yellow circles are **polar amino acids**.
- Red circles are **nonpolar amino acids**.
- The chain ends are marked NH₃⁺ and COO⁻.

> 📷 See **Slides 7 and 8** — Slide 7 shows the unfolded chain. A green box covers the folded result. Slide 8 shows the "Unfolded polypeptide," then "Protein folding," then the "Folded polypeptide."

## 9. Level 4 — Quaternary structure

**Quaternary structure is two or more chains fitting together into one protein.**

**What it is:**
- **2+ subunits** packed together.
- A **subunit** is one polypeptide chain in the protein *(Slide 6 notes: "two or more polypeptide chains")*.

**Key fact:**
- The slide says: **"*NOT ALL PROTEINS HAVE THIS!!!!"**
- A protein with only one chain stops at Level 3 *(Slide 6 notes)*.

**Kinds shown on the slide:**
- **Dimer:** 2 subunits.
- **Homodimer:** a dimer with 2 matching subunits.
- **Heterodimer:** a dimer with 2 different subunits.
- **Trimer:** 3 subunits. Example: **collagen**, drawn as three twisted strands.
- **Tetramer:** 4 subunits. Example: **hemoglobin**.
- The slide calls hemoglobin an "alpha-2-beta-2 tetramer."
- **Outside source:** Alpha-2-beta-2 means hemoglobin has 2 alpha subunits and 2 beta subunits. Alpha and beta are names for two kinds of chains. Example: it is like a chair with two pairs of matching legs. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- **Outside source:** Collagen is a protein that makes skin, bones, and tendons strong. Example: it helps your skin stretch and snap back. Example: gelatin in gummy candy comes from collagen. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*
- **Outside source:** Hemoglobin is a protein in red blood cells. It carries oxygen around your body. Example: it is why blood looks red. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> **Analogy:** Level 4 is like a **paragraph**. Several sentences work together, like several chains.

> 📷 See **Slide 9** — one pink folded chain, an arrow, then pink and yellow chains packed together. The dimer, trimer, and tetramer boxes are on the right.

## 11. Nucleic acids

**Nucleic acids store and pass on hereditary information.**

**Jobs:**
- They store and transmit **hereditary information**.
- They **encode protein products**.
- **Outside source:** Hereditary information is passed from parents to their children. To encode means to hold the code for something. Example: eye color can be passed from parents to children. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**What they are:**
- **Examples:** DNA and RNA.
- The monomer of nucleic acids is the **nucleotide**.

**The three parts of a nucleotide:**
- A **5-carbon sugar**.
- A **phosphate group**.
- A **nitrogenous base**.

**The bases:**
- **DNA:** A, T, C, G.
- **RNA:** A, U, C, G.
- A is **adenine**. G is **guanine**. C is **cytosine**.
- T is **thymine**. It is in DNA.
- U is **uracil**. It is in RNA.

**In the big drawing:**
- A strand of four nucleotides is linked together.
- Red circles are **phosphates**. Blue rings are the sugar **deoxyribose**.
- The top of the strand is labeled **5′ end**. The bottom is labeled **3′ end**.
- Adenine and guanine are labeled **purine bases**.
- Cytosine and thymine (in DNA) are labeled **pyrimidine bases**.
- A small box shows **ribose (in RNA)** and **uracil (in RNA)**.
- A green box covers a small spot at the bottom right of this drawing.

**In the small drawing:**
- One nucleotide is labeled **phosphate**, **ribose**, and **base**.

> **Why:** This links back to Level 1. A gene's DNA sets the order of amino acids in a protein.

## 12. DNA vs. RNA

**DNA and RNA share three bases, but each has one base of its own.**

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| **Full name** | Deoxyribonucleic acid | Ribonucleic acid |
| **Bases** | A, **T**, C, G | A, **U**, C, G |
| **Sugar** | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| **Strands** | Two (double helix) | One |

- DNA uses **T**, which stands for **thymine**.
- RNA uses **U**, which stands for **uracil**, in place of T.
- Both use **A** (adenine), **C** (cytosine), and **G** (guanine).
- The picture calls these bases **nucleobases**.

**In the picture:**
- Thymine has an arrow to DNA only.
- Adenine, cytosine, and guanine have arrows to both.
- Uracil has an arrow to RNA only.
- DeoxyRibose sits by DNA. Ribose sits by RNA.
- DNA is drawn as two twisted strands.
- RNA is drawn as one strand.
- Green boxes cover the labels above each name.

## 13. DNA structure: the double helix

**DNA is a double helix: two strands twisted around each other.**

**Outside and inside:**
- **Outside:** sugars and phosphates form the **sugar-phosphate backbone**.
- **Inside:** the bases reach into the middle in pairs, like the steps of a staircase.
- **Hydrogen bonds** hold the two bases of each pair together.

**Pairing rule:**
- A pairs with T.
- C pairs with G.
- The picture calls this **complementary base pairing**. Each base has one matching partner.

**Direction of the strands:**
- The two strands run in opposite directions.
- The **5′ end** of one strand pairs with the **3′ end** of the other.
- This is called **antiparallel**. The notes say it matters when DNA is copied.
- **Outside source:** You say 5′ as "five prime" and 3′ as "three prime." Example: "5′ end" is read "five prime end." *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the picture:**
- The left drawing is labeled "Double helix."
- The right drawing zooms in. It is labeled "Backbone" and "Complementary base pairing."
- P circles (phosphates) and sugars sit on the outside.
- A–T and C–G pairs sit in the middle.
- Dashed lines between each pair are the hydrogen bonds.
- A–T pairs have 2 dashed lines. C–G pairs have 3.

> **Analogy:** DNA is like a twisted ladder. The backbones are the rails. The base pairs are the rungs.

## Quick review

- Proteins regulate, transport, give structure, and control reaction rates (enzymes).
- The monomer of proteins is the amino acid. There are about 20.
- An amino acid has an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a side chain.
- The R-group, or side chain, is different in each amino acid.
- The chart groups side chains: nonpolar, polar, and electrically charged (acidic or basic).
- A peptide is about 2–50 amino acids. A polypeptide is about 51 or more.
- A protein is 1 or more polypeptides with a functional 3D structure.
- The four levels are primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary.
- Primary is the amino acid order, joined by covalent peptide bonds. It runs from N-terminus to C-terminus.
- Secondary is α helices and β pleated sheets, held by backbone hydrogen bonds.
- Tertiary comes from ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, disulfide bridges, and hydrophobic interactions.
- In water, nonpolar amino acids fold to the center. Polar ones stay outside.
- Quaternary is 2+ subunits, like dimers, trimers (collagen), and tetramers (hemoglobin). Not all proteins have it.
- The four levels match letters, words, sentences, and paragraphs.
- A nucleotide has a 5-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.
- Adenine and guanine are purine bases. Cytosine and thymine are pyrimidine bases.
- DNA uses A, T, C, G and deoxyribose. RNA uses A, U, C, G and ribose.
- DNA has two strands. RNA has one.
- In DNA, A pairs with T, and C pairs with G, held by hydrogen bonds.
- DNA's strands are antiparallel: 5′ end pairs with 3′ end.
- Slide 13 shows the backbone, base pairs, and nucleotides in more drawings.
