## 1. Monomers, polymers, and carbon

**Macromolecules: monomers + monomers = polymer!**

**The pieces:**
- Monomers join with other monomers.
- Many monomers joined together make a **polymer**.

> **Outside source:** "Macro" means big. A macromolecule is a giant molecule made of many smaller pieces. Example: starch, which hydrolysis breaks into glucose (Slide 9 notes). *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Carbon:**
- Carbon has **four** valence electrons.
- Carbon can form rings and chains.
- Carbon can bond to other carbon atoms.
- It can also bond to other elements.
- **Organic** molecules contain carbon.
- **Inorganic** molecules do not contain carbon.

> **Outside source:** Valence electrons are an atom's outermost electrons. An atom uses them to make bonds. Carbon has four, like a connector with four plugs. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the pictures:**
- Loose monomers join into one long polymer.
- The arrow from monomers to polymer is labeled **polymerization**.
- Three carbon molecules are drawn: a long chain (a), a small molecule (b), and a ring (c).

## 2. Energy and the laws of thermodynamics

**Energy is the ability to change or move matter.**

**What energy is:**
- Energy can convert from one form to another.
- The chart splits energy into two groups: **potential** and **kinetic**.

> **Outside source:** Potential energy is stored energy. A stretched rubber band has it. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion. A rolling ball has it. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the energy chart (top right):**
- Kinds of potential energy: chemical, gravitational, electrical, nuclear, and elastic.
- Kinds of kinetic energy: electrical, light, sound, thermal, and wind.
- Electrical shows up on both sides. Source Notes explains why.

**The two laws:**
- **1st law:** You can't destroy or create energy.
- **2nd law:** **Entropy** always increases.

**In the cannon picture (1st law):**
- The picture says "Energy Cannot Be Created or Destroyed (It just changes forms)."
- The cannon starts with **chemical energy**.
- When it fires, the ball has kinetic energy plus potential energy.
- As the ball flies up, it has less kinetic energy and more potential energy.
- When the ball lands, the energy turns into heat.
- The picture calls heat the "kinetic energy of molecules."

**In the dot picture (2nd law):**
- Neat rows of dots show low entropy. The slide calls it "highly ordered."
- Scattered dots show high entropy. The slide calls it "more disordered."
- The arrow between them says "increase in entropy."
- So more entropy means more disorder.

## 3. Chemical reactions

**In a chemical reaction, bonds break or form. The chemical itself changes.**

**Chemical or physical?**
- A **chemical reaction** breaks bonds, forms bonds, or both.
- A **physical change** is different.
- In a physical change, something changes state or gets cut into pieces.
- Melting, boiling, and freezing are physical changes.
- Many people think melting is a chemical reaction. It is NOT.
- For a chemical change, the chemical itself must change in some way.
- A melted ice cube is still water.
- Boiled water is still H₂O. It is just a gas now instead of a liquid.
- Breaking water into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas is a chemical change.
- That's because it is no longer H₂O.

**Reading a reaction:**
- The slide's reaction is 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.
- **Reactants** are on the left of the arrow.
- **Products** are on the right of the arrow.
- The **coefficient** is the number in front of a substance.
- It tells how many of that substance the reaction needs.
- Here, you need 2 molecules of H₂ and only 1 molecule of O₂.
- Together they form 2 molecules of H₂O.

**Letters in parentheses:**
- Some reactions have a letter in parentheses after each substance.
- The letter tells that substance's state of matter.
- (s) means solid.
- (l) means liquid.
- (g) means gas.
- (aq) means **aqueous**, which means dissolved.
- Example: O₂ (g) means oxygen gas.

**In the pictures:**
- Two H₂ molecules and one O₂ molecule become two H₂O molecules.
- A big red X covers ice melting into water.
- The X means melting is not a chemical reaction.

> 📷 See **Slide 4** — the reaction with reactants and products, and the red X over melting ice.

## 4. Exothermic and endothermic reactions

**Some reactions release heat. Others absorb heat.**

**The two kinds:**
- An **exothermic** reaction releases energy as heat.
- An **endothermic** reaction absorbs energy from its surroundings as heat.
- Exothermic reactions can happen on their own. The notes call this "spontaneously."
- Endothermic reactions can't happen unless energy is put in.

**In the pictures:**
- Exothermic: a fire with heat arrows pointing out.
- The exothermic reaction is hotter than its surroundings.
- Endothermic: an ice cube with heat arrows pointing in.
- The endothermic reaction is cooler than its surroundings.

> **Memory trick:** **Exo** sounds like **exit**, so heat leaves. **Endo** sounds like **enter**, so heat comes in.

> 📷 See **Slide 5** — "Heat released" (fire) or "Heat absorbed" (ice cube).

## 5. Potential energy diagrams

**A potential energy diagram shows if a reaction is exothermic or endothermic.**

**How to read it:**
- The up-and-down axis is labeled **potential energy**.
- Slide 6 labels the across axis "reaction coordinate."
- Slide 7 labels the across axis "reaction progress."
- Look at the energy level of the products versus the reactants.
- Products lower than reactants: "energy released." It's **exothermic**.
- Products higher than reactants: "energy absorbed." It's **endothermic**.

**In the pictures:**
- Slide 6: the line climbs over a hump. Then it ends lower than it started.
- Slide 7, left graph: an exothermic reaction.
- Its products sit below its reactants. A red arrow says "energy released."
- Slide 7, right graph: an endothermic reaction.
- Its products sit above its reactants. A red arrow says "energy absorbed."
- Both graphs also have a blue arrow labeled "activation energy."
- The blue arrow goes from the reactants up to the top of the hump.

> **Outside source:** Activation energy is the energy a reaction needs to get started. Think of pushing a sled up a hill before it can slide down. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> **Your teacher's note on Slide 7:** “We will talk more about the ‘activation energy’ concept in a future unit, for now we are just focusing on the exo/endo thermic ideas (energy releasing/absorbing)”

## 6. Building and breaking molecules

**Building big molecules uses energy. Breaking them apart releases energy.**

**Anabolism and catabolism (Slide 8):**
- **Anabolism** creates large molecules from smaller molecules. It uses energy.
- **Catabolism** breaks large molecules into smaller molecules. It releases energy.

**In the Slide 8 picture:**
- "Anabolic: Small molecules are assembled into large ones. Energy is required."
- Four separate circles plus energy become one chain.
- "Catabolic: Large molecules are broken down into small ones. Energy is released."
- One chain breaks into four circles, plus energy.

**Water out, water in (Slide 9):**
- **Dehydration synthesis** builds up molecules by losing water molecules.
- **Hydrolysis** splits a compound into pieces by adding water.
- Hydrolysis is used to break polymers into simpler units.
- Example from the notes: starch breaks into glucose.

**In the Slide 9 picture:**
- Dehydration synthesis: two rings join together.
- An OH comes off one ring. An H comes off the other.
- Those pieces leave as water (H₂O).
- Hydrolysis: water is added where the two rings join.
- The rings split apart again.
- The slide labels hydrolysis "digestion."

> **Outside source:** "Hydro" means water, and "lysis" means splitting. "De-" means taking away. You already know this: being dehydrated means your body lost water. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> 📷 See **Slides 8 and 9** — circles joining and breaking, and water leaving or being added.

## 7. ATP, the cell's energy currency

**ATP is the "energy currency of the cell."**

**What ATP is (Slide 10):**
- ATP stands for **adenosine triphosphate**.
- The slide underlines A, T, and P in the name. That's where "ATP" comes from.
- ATP has three phosphate groups.
- It has one **ribose** sugar.
- It has one **adenine**, which the slide calls a nitrogenous base.

> **Outside source:** "Nitrogenous" means it contains nitrogen. You can spot it in the drawing: each N is a nitrogen atom. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**ATP hydrolysis (Slide 12):**
- ATP + H₂O → Pi + ADP + energy.
- **ADP** stands for adenosine diphosphate. The picture shows it with two phosphates.
- **Pi** stands for inorganic phosphate. It's the one phosphate that broke off.
- In the picture, each circled P is a phosphate.
- The slide title calls this "ATP Hydrolysis," because water splits ATP.

**The ATP cycle (Slide 13):**
- ATP is the charged battery.
- Water (H₂O) goes in, and ATP splits into ADP + Pi.
- This step releases energy.
- ADP + Pi is the dead battery.
- Turning ADP + Pi back into ATP requires energy.
- In that step, water (H₂O) comes out.

> 📷 See **Slides 10, 12, and 13** — ATP's three parts, ATP splitting, and the battery cycle.

## 8. Lipids

**Lipids store energy for the long term.**

- **Made of:** carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen (CHO).
- **Building blocks:** fatty acids and glycerol.
- **Found in:** oils, butter, dairy, and waxes.
- **Job:** long-term energy storage, 9 **kcal** per gram.

> **Outside source:** A kcal is a unit of energy in food. The "Calories" on a food label are kcal. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Jobs in the cell (Slide 16):**
- Lipids form the **phospholipid bilayer**. That is the cell membrane.
- Lipids store energy.
- Lipids provide insulation.
- Lipids aid in cell signaling.

> **Outside source:** Insulation means keeping heat in, like a winter coat. Cell signaling means cells sending messages to each other, like passing notes. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the Slide 14 pictures:**
- Three kinds of lipids are shown: **phospholipid**, **triglyceride**, and **cholesterol**.
- Phospholipids line up in two layers around a cell.
- That double layer is labeled "phospholipid bilayer (cell membrane)."
- A **triglyceride** is one glycerol plus three fatty acids.
- Each fatty acid is drawn as a carbon chain.

**In the Slide 16 pictures:**
- An **omega-3 phospholipid** is shown.
- An arrow shows it fitting into the cell membrane lipid bilayer.
- Other labels: cell, cell membrane, phospholipid, and protein.
- The protein sits inside the bilayer.

**Food photos (Slide 15):** butter and a glass of milk.

**Compare:** A gram of lipid holds 9 kcal. A gram of carbohydrate holds 4 kcal (Slide 20).

> 📷 See **Slides 14 and 16** — the three lipid types, the triglyceride, and the membrane bilayer.

## 9. Carbohydrates

**Carbohydrates ("carbs") store energy for the short term.**

- **Made of:** carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen (CHO) in a 1:2:1 ratio.
- **Building blocks:** **monosaccharides**.
- **Name clue:** the suffix identifier is **-ose**.
- **Found in:** sugar, bread, pasta, rice, and fruits.
- **Job:** short-term energy storage, 4 kcal per gram.

**Jobs in the cell (Slide 20):**
- Carbs form cell walls.
- Carbs provide energy.
- Carbs help with cell recognition.

> **Outside source:** Cell recognition means cells identifying each other. Carbs on a cell work a bit like name tags. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the Slide 18 pictures:**
- "The Structure of Glucose" shows glucose three ways.
- It's drawn as a straight chain, as a ring, and as a 3-D model.
- A **monosaccharide** is drawn as one ring.
- A **disaccharide** is drawn as two rings joined together.
- A **polysaccharide** is drawn as many rings in a big, branching chain.

> **Outside source:** "Mono" means one, "di" means two, and "poly" means many. "Saccharide" means sugar. You see "mono" in monorail (one rail) and "poly" in polygon (many sides). *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**In the Slide 20 picture:**
- Glucose "gets converted to" ATP.
- The ATP is labeled "energy."

**Food photos (Slides 19 and 20):** rice, candy, and fruit.

> 📷 See **Slides 18 and 20** — glucose, the mono/di/polysaccharide shapes, and glucose turning into ATP.

## 10. Proteins

**Proteins give structural support and form enzymes.**

- **Made of:** carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur (CHON(S)).
- **Building blocks:** amino acids.
- **Name clue:** the suffix identifier is **-ase**. Source Notes explains more.
- **Found in:** meat, eggs, beans, tofu, dairy, and nuts.
- **Job:** secondary energy source and structural support.

> **Outside source:** "Secondary" means second in line, like a backup plan. *(Merriam-Webster)*

**Jobs in the cell (Slide 24):**
- Proteins form **enzymes**.
- Proteins aid in cell transport.
- Proteins give structural support.
- Proteins help with signaling.
- Proteins help with immune defense.

> **Outside source:** An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction. It's like a helper that makes a job go faster. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> **Outside source:** Cell transport means moving things into and out of cells. It's like doors letting people in and out of a building. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

> **Outside source:** Immune defense means your body fighting off germs. It's what happens when you get over a cold. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Parts of an amino acid (Slide 22 picture):**
- A carbon (C) sits in the middle.
- An **amine group** (H–N–H) is on one side.
- A **carboxyl group** (C=O with OH) is on the other side.
- An H sits on top.
- The **variable group**, labeled **R**, is on the bottom.

> **Outside source:** "Variable" means it changes. The R group is different in each kind of amino acid. It's like keys cut from the same blank, each with different teeth. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**The protein chain (Slide 22 picture):**
- A protein is drawn as a long chain of amino acids, like beads on a string.
- A **peptide bond** label points to the link between amino acids.
- The two ends of the chain are labeled **N-terminus** and **C-terminus**.
- Small labels on the beads say Leu, Phe, Ser, and Cys.

> **Outside source:** Leu, Phe, Ser, and Cys are short nicknames for amino acids. It's like writing "Sept." for September. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Digestive enzymes (Slide 24 picture):**
- The chart is titled "Functions of Key Digestive Enzymes."
- Enzymes for bread: amylase, sucrase-isomaltase, maltase, and lactase.
- Enzymes for meat: pepsin, trypsin, and peptidase.
- The enzyme for oil: lipase.
- Each enzyme's arrow leads to small pieces of the food.
- Most of these enzyme names end in -ase.

**Muscle picture (Slide 24):**
- Labels: myofiber, myofibril, actin, troponin, tropomyosin, and myosin.

> **Outside source:** A myofiber is one muscle fiber, and myofibrils are thinner threads inside it. Actin and myosin are proteins that slide past each other to shorten the muscle. Troponin and tropomyosin work like an on/off switch for that. You use them when you lift a backpack. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Food photos (Slide 23):** a spread of protein foods and a tofu dish.

## 11. Nucleic acids

**Nucleic acids store and carry genetic information.**

- **Made of:** carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and phosphorus (CHONP).
- **Building blocks:** **nucleotides**.
- **Found in:** any living cells, like meats, plants, and mushrooms.
- They are in those cells as DNA and RNA.
- **Job:** information storage.
- They also code and transport genetic information.

**Jobs in the cell (Slide 28):**
- **DNA** stores genetic information.
- **RNA** is involved with protein synthesis.

> **Outside source:** "Synthesis" means making or putting together. So protein synthesis means making proteins, like building with blocks. *(OpenStax Biology 2e)*

**Parts of a nucleotide (Slide 26 picture):**
- A **phosphate**.
- A sugar. This picture labels it **ribose**.
- A **base**. The pictures also call bases "nucleobases."

**The bases (Slides 26, 27, and 28 pictures):**
- DNA bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C).
- RNA bases: adenine (A), uracil (U), guanine (G), and cytosine (C).
- DNA has thymine. RNA has uracil in its place.
- Slide 27 shows **base pairs** in DNA.
- Adenine pairs with thymine.
- Guanine pairs with cytosine.
- The long sides of DNA are labeled **sugar phosphate backbone**.
- Slide 26 labels those sides "helix of sugar-phosphates."

**DNA vs. RNA (Slides 26 and 28 pictures):**
- The Slide 28 picture also shows the label **single nucleobase**.

| | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Full name | Deoxyribonucleic acid | Ribonucleic acid |
| Strands | Double-stranded | Usually single-stranded |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Base only in this one | Thymine | Uracil |

- Both have cytosine, guanine, and adenine.
- The BONUS picture on Slide 28 shows deoxyribose next to ribose.
- Deoxyribose has an H in one spot. Ribose has an OH there.

**Slide 29 cartoon:** "A DNA strand like me is a blueprint for building a living thing!"

**Food photo (Slide 27):** a table of many foods.

## Compare the four biomolecules

**Same pattern for each: what it's made of, its building block, and its job.**

| Biomolecule | Building block | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| **Lipids** (CHO) | Fatty acids & glycerol | Long-term energy, 9 kcal/g |
| **Carbohydrates** (CHO, 1:2:1) | Monosaccharides (-ose) | Short-term energy, 4 kcal/g |
| **Proteins** (CHON(S)) | Amino acids (-ase) | Secondary energy, structural support |
| **Nucleic acids** (CHONP) | Nucleotides | Store and carry genetic information |

## Quick review

- Monomers join to make polymers. Carbon has four valence electrons. Organic means it contains carbon.
- Energy can't be created or destroyed. Entropy always increases. Energy is potential or kinetic.
- A chemical reaction breaks or forms bonds. Melting is only a physical change.
- Exothermic releases heat and can happen on its own. Endothermic absorbs heat and needs energy.
- On an energy diagram, products lower means exothermic. Products higher means endothermic.
- Activation energy is the blue arrow up to the top of the hump.
- Anabolism builds and uses energy. Catabolism breaks and releases energy.
- Dehydration synthesis builds (water out). Hydrolysis breaks (water in).
- ATP has three phosphates, ribose, and adenine. ATP + water → ADP + Pi + energy.
- Lipids: CHO, fatty acids and glycerol, 9 kcal/g, long-term energy, cell membrane.
- Carbs: CHO 1:2:1, monosaccharides, -ose, 4 kcal/g, short-term energy, cell walls.
- Proteins: CHON(S), amino acids, -ase, enzymes, structural support, secondary energy.
- Nucleic acids: CHONP, nucleotides, DNA stores genetic information, RNA does protein synthesis.
